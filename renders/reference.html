Basement Wall Divider — Build Reference

Overview Bedroom elevation Office elevation Plan view Upper panel section Ladder framing Build reference

What we want built

Close the opening between the bedroom and the office with a divider that reads as a wall, using two IKEA KALLAX bookcases we already own — one opening into each room, backed so you cannot see or shine light through.

The three things that matter:

  • It must block light. Someone sleeps on one side while the other side is in use.
  • It must feel solid — no wobble when you lean on it or pull a book off a shelf.
  • It should come out again without rebuilding the room. Screws into studs and joists are fine; we just don't want it glued to the house.

Soundproofing is not a requirement. Don't spend money or complexity on it.

How it goes together: a plywood ladder frame (bottom plate, three vertical stiles, top plate) fills the opening and is screwed to the wall studs at each side. The two KALLAX units drop into the two bays as infill — they are not structural. Each gets a thin plywood backer on the face opposite its open side, then board-and-batten trim over the whole thing. Above the bookcases, a plywood panel closes the remaining gap to the ceiling.

These drawings are a proven-out design, not a cut sheet. They exist to show the idea works and that everything fits. Re-measure everything and let your own numbers win. If you see a better way to build any of it, we're glad to hear it — the only things we're firm on are the three goals above. A few decisions do have non-obvious reasons behind them, so those are written up under Why it's drawn this way at the bottom before you change them.

Which wall is which

Standing in either room facing the divider, "left" and "right" flip between rooms — the drawings on this site call the two walls by what they actually are instead, and every page below uses these names consistently:

  • External wall — the foundation wall. This is the bedroom's left wall and the office's right wall — the same physical wall, seen from opposite ends of the opening. The office Alex/desktop run's tall-Alex end sits flush against this wall.
  • Internal wall — the interior partition wall. This is the bedroom's right wall and the office's left wall — also the same physical wall. The bedroom desk sits flush against this wall; the office run's desk end trails toward it but stops ~12" short.

Materials

  • 3/4" plywood — 2–3 sheets: plates, centre stile, splice scab, centre-line blocking
  • 1/2" plywood, paint-grade — 1 sheet: upper panel
  • 1/4" plywood, paint-grade — 3 sheets: backers
  • 1x4 primed pine — ~6 sticks of 8': stile battens, and the outer stiles if you rip those from pine
  • 1x3 primed pine — ~5 sticks: backer centre battens and the two cap rails
  • 1x2 primed pine — ~3 sticks: upper panel ceiling trim and seam cap strip
  • Baseboard — ~18 linear ft, matching each room's existing profile
  • Screws — construction screws for studs; washer-head or large-head for the bookcase-to-stile connection
  • Paintable caulk — for the light sealing above

Plywood is paint-grade birch or poplar core — we're painting everything, and it handles basement humidity better than MDF.

Fastening

  • Outer stiles to the wall: construction screws into the studs (not nails), driven from the room side before the backer goes on. Long enough to bite ~1 1/4" into the stud.
  • Top plate to stiles: 3–4 #8 x 2" screws through each stile face into the plate, glued.
  • Plate splices: stagger the top and bottom splices so they aren't on the same vertical line. A ~16" plywood scab glued and screwed over the top plate's splice.
  • Bookcases to the stiles: 4–6 screws per unit through the carcass side. Just enough to stop it shifting — not structural. Use washer-head or large-head screws; a bare screw head pulls straight through KALLAX board. Shim the gap at each screw first so you aren't crushing the carcass sideways.
  • Ceiling brackets: two steel angle brackets (2" x 2" x 1 1/2", 16 ga), one per face, at the top of the centre-line blocking run. Into a joist if one lands within ~6" of centre; otherwise toggle anchors rated 50 lb+ shear. This is what stops the middle of the divider from wobbling — the wall screws only hold the two ends.
  • Battens and cap rail: brads plus a little glue. Cap rail gets a caulk bead along its lower edge (see Light sealing).
  • Track light junction box: a track light crosses the opening near the ceiling step, with its junction box on the bedroom side. The upper panel's edge is placed just past the box so the box stays exposed and accessible — no cutting into it, which the electrical code requires. Needs a field measurement before the panel is cut (see Still open).

Light sealing

Blocking light is a primary goal, and the wall surfaces alone don't do it — light gets through the perimeter gaps. Caulk or a foam strip at each of these, during assembly rather than after:

  • Under the bottom plate — shimming a slightly uneven floor leaves gaps, and light travels along floors.
  • At the ceiling — ceilings are never flat over 100". The 1x2 ceiling trim covers it; caulk it rather than only nailing.
  • Both side gaps — where the outer stile meets the wall, full height.
  • Under the cap rail — easiest one to miss. The frame is built to the tallest bookcase, so a shorter one leaves a slot at its top. The rail hides it; the caulk stops the light.

One thing we've accepted: the KALLAX cubbies are open, so each one glows slightly from whichever room it opens into. The assembly is opaque overall — that's just inherent to using open bookcases.

Finish

  • Bedroom-facing surfaces (4x4 backer, board & batten): painted very light pink. KALLAX units on the bedroom side: white.
  • Office-facing surfaces (4x3 backer, board & batten): color still undecided.
  • Baseboard: matches each room's existing profile, run across the bottom plate edge.
  • Nothing gets mounted to the backers — posters taped only, no anchors or hooks.

Still open — needs a decision or a measurement

  • Needs measuring: ceiling joist direction and locations. This is the one that could change the plan — it determines whether the ceiling brackets can hit a joist or have to use anchors. Quick check with a stud finder.
  • Needs measuring: wall stud locations on both sides, so the outer stiles land on something.
  • Needs measuring: the track light junction box — how far it hangs below the ceiling and where it sits. The upper panel can't be cut until we know; if it turns out not to clear, we'll trim around it instead.
  • Our decision: paint colour for the office-facing side. Bedroom side is very light pink; office side is undecided.

Everything else that was open has been folded into the drawings. Normal re-measuring of the opening isn't listed here — assume you'll do that for everything.

Why it's drawn this way

Background only — you don't need any of this to build it. It's here so that if something looks like an odd choice, you can check whether there was a reason before changing it. Open whichever is relevant.

The design decisions and their reasoning

The one constraint everything else follows from

The opening is about 105 1/4" wide. The two KALLAX units, sitting side by side, are 101 13/16" of that. Which leaves:

Opening, wall to wall105 1/4"
KALLAX 4x3 + KALLAX 4x4− 101 13/16"
Everything else must fit in3 7/16"

That 3 7/16" has to cover three vertical members and all the fitting slack. It is a fixed pie: width spent on one thing is taken from another. Nearly every decision below is a choice about how to spend it.

Why the bays are cut oversized (the most important idea here)

An earlier version of this design sized the frame as an exact sum of the measured KALLAX widths. That meant every measurement error, and every bit of bow in a particleboard carcass, accumulated into one go/no-go dimension — and it came out with about 1/8" of slack across 104". That is not a build tolerance, it is a coin flip, and it would have been discovered with the wood already cut.

This version does the opposite. Each bay is cut 1/2" wider than its unit, so the KALLAX drops in loose and can be slid to wherever it sits square. The gap that leaves is then covered by the battens, which were already in the design for other reasons. So:

  • Bay for the 4x3: ~44 1/2" (unit is 44")
  • Bay for the 4x4: ~58 5/16" (unit is 57 13/16")

A gap that trim hides costs nothing. A frame that will not go in costs a weekend. If your measurements differ from ours, give the difference to the bay play rather than trying to hit a number exactly.

How the battens absorb the slack

Worst case, a unit is pushed hard to one end of its bay and the entire 1/2" of play shows up as a single gap at the other end. The batten centred on that stile has to cover its own half of the stile, cross that gap, and still land on the unit with something to spare:

BattenLands on the unit byVerdict
1x2 (1 1/2")nothingnot an option
1x3 (2 1/2")3/8"the bare minimum
1x4 (3 1/2")7/8"what this design uses

At the walls the same batten laps the unit by over 2", so the walls were never the tight case — the centre stile is what sets the batten width.

Why the outer stile and wall cleat are one piece

The earlier design had two stacked 3/4" members at each wall: a cleat screwed to the studs, then a stile screwed to the face of that cleat. They do different jobs on paper but occupy the same place, so they have been merged into a single member (~27/32") that screws to the studs, takes the backer edge, and passes in front of the baseboard so nothing needs notching.

That reclaims 3/4" at each wall — 1 1/2" total, which is exactly what pays for the bay play above. Without this merge there is not enough width for meaningful slack.

Rip this member to whatever thickness actually clears the baseboard. If your baseboard is thicker than ours, take the extra out of the bay play — do not grow the frame, which has nowhere to go.

Why the frame is plywood, not dimensional lumber

Two reasons, and the second is the one that matters.

Nothing standard is wide enough. The frame members are 15 15/16" deep, to match the KALLAX carcass plus a backer each face. The widest common board is a 1x12 at 11 1/4" actual — over 4" short. Getting full depth from lumber means gluing up two boards per stile: more work, more cost, and a glue line that can cup. Ripping a plywood sheet is easier and comes out flat.

The depth is doing the structural work. Resistance to someone pushing on the divider scales with the cube of the member's depth into the room. That makes the comparison lopsided:

MemberDepth into the roomRelative stiffness
Full-depth plywood stile15 15/16"3679
2x4 laid flat3 1/2"43
2x4 on edge, pushed sideways1 1/2"3

The plywood stile is roughly 86× stiffer against tipping than a flat 2x4. Substituting lumber here would throw that away and make the ceiling-restraint problem harder, not easier.

Where lumber is the right call: the outer stiles (screwed flat to the wall, cantilevering nothing — 1x4 pine ripped to depth is easier than ripping plywood) and the upper framing above the top plate, where there is no carcass to match and 2x3/2x4 is simply easier to work with.

Why the centre stile was not widened

Doubling the centre stile to 1 1/2" was considered and rejected. Widening it along the wall does not change its depth into the room, which is the dimension resisting a push — so it buys better screw-holding and splice bearing, but almost no tipping resistance. Meanwhile it costs 3/4" out of the same 3 7/16" budget, which would cut the bay play roughly in half.

Paying real slack for no structural gain is a bad trade. The tipping problem is solved at the ceiling instead (see "Structural review findings").

Why there is a cap rail

A horizontal 1x3 runs the full width on both room faces, at the top of the KALLAX where the top plate is. It looks like trim, and it is the last piece anyone would think to add — but it is closing four separate gaps that nothing else in the design covers.

  1. A bare plywood edge at eye level. The top plate presents a raw 3/4" sheet edge to each room, about 105" long. Every other visible surface on these faces is finished — paint-grade backer, primed pine batten — and then there is exposed edge grain running the entire width.
  2. A light path — the one that actually matters. The frame is built to the tallest measured KALLAX (57 11/16"), but the units measured 57 5/8"–57 11/16". A shorter unit therefore sits up to 1/16" below the top plate, leaving an open slot the full width of that bookcase, right at the top. Blocking light is a primary requirement of this build, so that is a real defect rather than a cosmetic one.
  3. The backer's cut top edge is otherwise exposed where it meets the top plate.
  4. The vertical battens would terminate in mid-air. Board-and-batten runs its verticals into a cap rail; without one, six battens simply stop.

Why 1x3 and not 1x4 or 1x2. The rail has to cover the 3/4" plate edge and still reach far enough down the KALLAX face to swallow the height variance:

StockLaps onto the unit byCosts of the top cubby's opening
1x2 (1 1/2")3/4"5%
1x3 (2 1/2")1 3/4"12%
1x4 (3 1/2")2 3/4"19%

On the backer face there are no openings, so any size works. On the open-shelf face the rail crosses the top row of cubbies — 1x4 takes a noticeable bite out of that row when loading books, and 1x2 laps only 3/4" onto the unit, which is thin margin against the height variance. 1x3 is the balance, and it matches the backer centre battens so the horizontal reads as a deliberate member rather than an odd size.

The battens run up into the rail and stop. The cap rail is one continuous piece per face; the six verticals are cut to die beneath it. That is standard board-and-batten practice and the simplest to execute — the alternative (verticals full height, rail chopped into segments between them) means more pieces and more joints to align for no gain.

Caulk the rail's lower edge where it meets the KALLAX face. That bead is what actually seals the height-variance gap — the rail covers it visually, the caulk stops the light. See "Light sealing".

Why the upper panel got simpler

The panel above the bookcases blocks light between the rooms. It holds nothing up, nothing hangs from it, and nothing sits on it. An earlier version of this design had it at 14 separate pieces — three panel pieces, four cleat runs, three vertical blocking runs, two seam cap strips, two brackets. That is a lot of parts for a light barrier, and two of those items turned out to be solving problems that did not exist.

The bow that was not going to happen. The old reasoning was that "a 102"-wide sheet held only top and bottom will bow over that span", so vertical blocking was added behind each seam. But that has the span backwards. The panel is captured in a cleat channel along its entire top edge and its entire bottom edge, on both faces. The unsupported dimension is not the 103" width — it is the panel's height between those cleats, which is 14 1/16" in the low zone and 22 15/16" in the tall zone. Half-inch plywood does not bow over 14 inches. For comparison, 1/2" drywall spans 16"–24" between studs held only at its edges and stays flat. A sheet cannot bow out-of-plane in mid-span when both long edges are continuously held, unless something pushes it — and nothing pushes this. The seam blocking is deleted.

The third panel piece that was not needed. The panel is ~103 9/16" wide, which exceeds a 96" sheet, so sheet size forces exactly one seam. It does not force a second. As a single piece the whole low zone is ~75 21/32" × ~14 1/16" — comfortably inside a 4x8 sheet. The second seam existed only to give the seam blocking a mid-span place to land; with the blocking gone it had no job. Two panel pieces, one seam, one cap strip.

Both pieces stay plain rectangles because the one remaining seam lands exactly at the ceiling step. That was already the right call and has not changed.

What deliberately did not get simplified

  • Still 1/2" plywood, not the backers' 1/4". The backers are fully supported by the KALLAX carcass behind them; this panel is a free membrane with nothing behind it. Over the tall zone's 22 15/16" height, 1/4" gives a ~92:1 span-to-thickness ratio — it would oil-can and drum whenever touched, and it will be touched, because there is a ledge directly below it. At 1/2" the ratio is ~46:1, which feels solid.
  • Still four cleat runs. These could drop to two by screwing the panel to a single cleat face, but the channel is what lets the panel simply drop into place instead of being held and fastened, and it seals light on both faces without relying on caulk. The stock is cheap and completely hidden. Not worth trading away for ~17 feet of 1x2.
  • Still centred in the depth. That yields 7 23/32" of ledge open to each room — a genuine usable shelf on both sides. That is a feature, not an accident of the geometry.
  • The centre-line blocking run and its ceiling brackets stay. These were never part of the panel system. That run is the tipping load path — the thing that keeps a push near the middle of the divider from having nowhere to go. See "Structural review findings".

Net: 14 pieces down to 9, with no loss of function. The parts that came out were the ones added to fix a misdiagnosed bow.

Why the plates splice in different places

The top and bottom plates are each two pieces, because no single board spans 105" conveniently. Earlier both spliced at the same point, over the centre stile — which puts a joint in both plates on the same vertical line and creates a hinge right at the frame's midpoint, the spot with the longest load path back to either wall.

Stagger them. Put the bottom plate's splice a foot or two away from the top plate's. It costs nothing, and it means the frame has continuous material in at least one plate at every point along its width.

Measurement notes — what we measured and what surprised us

Field measurements (KALLAX units already in place against the walls) turned up a few small differences from the original nominal numbers. None of them threaten the fit — the assembly still clears the opening with real margin — but they're worth knowing about:

  • Both KALLAX units measure slightly larger than the IKEA spec sheet: 4x3 is 44" wide (spec: 43 7/8"), 4x4 is up to 57 13/16" wide at the bottom of the carcass (spec: 57 7/8"), and both are 15 7/16" deep (spec: 15 3/8") and up to 57 11/16" tall (spec: 57 5/8"). The cut list and every drawing on this site use these measured numbers.
  • There is no casing on this opening. The side walls are bare drywall corners at standing height; the only trim is the baseboard at the floor (confirmed visually — see photos/baseboard.jpg — and by direct inspection of both sides). Earlier versions of this page carried a "casing ~3/4" proud each side" assumption inherited from prompt.md, and treated the floor-level baseboard-to-baseboard reading as an "inside casing" constraint the frame had to fit inside. Both were wrong. See "Structural review findings".
  • Wall-to-wall at KALLAX height is 105 1/4" (corrected Sept 2026 — an earlier 106 1/8" reading was superseded). Once the casing assumption is dropped, the two field readings are consistent and their difference is simply the baseboard:
    wall to wall (bare drywall, at KALLAX top) = 105 1/4"
    baseboard face to baseboard face (at floor) = 104 3/16"
    difference ÷ 2 = ~17/32" — the baseboard's projection
    That derived ~17/32" is close to the 5/8" baseboard originally assumed in prompt.md, which is a good sign. The former "unexplained ~2" discrepancy" was an artifact of the superseded reading plus the phantom casing, and needs no explanation.
  • The frame's real width constraint is the bare wall-to-wall 105 1/4", not the floor reading — which is taken below the bottom plate and never constrains the frame. Against 105 1/4" the 104 1/16" frame has 1 3/16" of total slack, or 19/32" per side. Workable, but not generous: it pins the side gap almost exactly, leaving little freedom to choose it.
  • Open — measure directly before cutting cleats: the baseboard's projection is currently derived by subtracting two readings taken at different heights, which compounds both their errors into the single number the side gap depends on. Hook a tape on the baseboard face and measure back to the wall above it. If it comes in thicker than ~19/32", the frame does not clear it as drawn under "Structural review findings".
  • Ceiling step measured at 28 3/4" from the external wall (design used 28 1/2"); tall-side ceiling height 82 1/8" (design: 82"); low-side ceiling height 73 1/4"–73 1/2" across a few points (design: 73") — this site uses the low end (73 1/4") as the worst case.
  • Floor is pretty level across the run; the 4x4 wobbles slightly — check and shim when setting the bottom plate.
  • Still open / not yet measured: wall stud-to-drywall-face thickness (needed to know how far the assembly projects into each room), stud locations on both walls, and the track light junction box's projection past the wall plane.
Structural review findings (what an earlier review caught and changed)

An independent structural/design review (Sept 2026) checked the whole plan for soundness, internal consistency, and constructability before this went to a handyman. Findings below, grouped by what's been resolved and what's still open. The two most consequential fixes — the top-plate splice reinforcement and the center-stile ceiling brackets — are now folded into the cut list and fastening decisions above.

Resolved

  • Out-of-plane tipping resistance (was Critical): the ladder frame was tied to the building only at its two end wall-cleats, 104" apart, with zero connection from the center stile / center 104" span to the ceiling — a push anywhere near the middle had no real load path back to structure, and the KALLAX units are deliberately not fastened to the stiles so they can't help. Screwing the top plate more firmly to the stiles doesn't fix this: the top plate is two pieces butt-spliced at the center stile (the exact point with the longest, weakest load path), so it can't act as a single stiff beam distributing a center-span load out to the two end cleats — the splice itself is the weak link, not the stile joints. Fix (corrected): an earlier version of this fix specified brackets "at the top of the center stile" — wrong, because the center stile stops at the top plate (~59 3/16" high) exactly like the other two stiles, and the ceiling above it is 73 1/4" (the center stile's x-position is past the ceiling step, so it's in the low zone, not the 82 1/8" tall zone). There is nothing between the top plate and the ceiling there except the upper panel itself. The actual fix: a third vertical blocking run (same 3/4" x 1 1/2" spec as the panel's seam blocking) at the center stile's x-position, running top plate to ceiling (~14 1/16"), with the two steel angle brackets at its top, not the stile's. Force path: a push on the divider center transfers through the KALLAX carcass (unfastened, so it only transmits contact force, not stiffness) into the top plate (one continuous member across all 3 stile joints) into this new blocking run into the ceiling bracket into a joist or drywall anchor. See "Fastening decisions" and the cut list.
  • Top plate splice unreinforced (was Critical): the splice lands on only 3/8" of stile edge grain on each side with nothing else at the most-loaded joint in the frame. Fix: a 3/4" plywood scab (~16" long) glued and screwed across the top of the splice. See the cut list.
  • Upper panel "has no intermediate vertical support" (was Important — withdrawn, the finding was wrong): this review flagged that a ~102"-wide sheet held only at its top and bottom edges would bow, and added vertical blocking behind each seam to fix it. On re-examination the premise does not hold. The panel is captured in a cleat channel along its entire top and bottom edge, on both faces — so the unsupported span is its height between the cleats (14 1/16" low zone, 22 15/16" tall zone), not its 103" width. Half-inch plywood does not bow over 14"–23" with both long edges continuously restrained; 1/2" drywall spans 16"–24" between studs held only at its edges. Resolution: the seam blocking is deleted, and with it the extra panel seam that existed to give the blocking somewhere to land. See "Why the upper panel got simpler".
  • Upper panel seams crossed the ceiling step (was Important): seams previously placed at flat 48"/96" marks crossed the ceiling step at ~28 3/4" from the opening's left edge, making one panel piece an L-shaped cutout with an unspecified sheet layout. Fix: the seam moved to land exactly at the step, so both panel pieces (~27 29/32" and ~75 21/32") are plain rectangles. (This finding stands — only the piece count changed, from three to two, when the mid-span seam was later removed along with the blocking it served.) See the updated cut list.
  • prompt.md vs. geometry.js disagreement (was Critical): prompt.md was the original handoff brief, written before field measurements — it now disagrees with the measured numbers in geometry.js on nearly every dimension (opening width, KALLAX sizes, stile length, upper panel material). Fix: prompt.md now carries an explicit stale-data warning at the top; geometry.js and this page are the only sources to cut from. See CLAUDE.md.
  • Stale hardcoded dimension labels in the elevation drawings (Minor): four height labels in elevation.js (base/carcass/ledge/upper-panel heights) were typed from the old prompt.md numbers instead of computed from the live constants like every other label on the page, so the bedroom/office elevations displayed measurements that quietly contradicted the cut list. Fix: all four now use inchLabel() against the same constants the cut list uses. Same fix applied to a stale divider-depth figure in framing.html's frame note.

Still open

  • Frame-to-opening fit — resolved by design, no longer a risk: this used to be the project's biggest open question. The frame was sized as an exact sum of the measured KALLAX widths, leaving ~1/8" of total slack across 104" against a single floor-level reading — a coin flip that would have been discovered with the wood already cut. The design now absorbs the uncertainty instead of trying to out-measure it: each bay is cut 1/2" wider than its unit, the outer stile and wall cleat are merged into one member to pay for that play, and 1x4 battens cover the resulting reveal. See "Read this first — how this design thinks".
